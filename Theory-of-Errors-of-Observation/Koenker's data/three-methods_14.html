An astronomer's transit timing is nothing like this: there each observation is already a measurement.

If the subject had a genuine threshold — a difference below which he truly cannot tell — what shape should these points make?

A true threshold predicts a corner: flat at chance, then rising. The data have no corner. Performance climbs smoothly out of chance from the smallest difference upward, which means the subject does better than guessing at differences he would tell you he cannot feel. Peirce and Jastrow built an experiment on precisely this, and it is visible inside Fechner's own method.

STAGE 03

Certainty is a free parameter

The subject has been answering a question that was never made precise. Say so when you suspect a difference and the threshold falls; say so only when certain and it rises. Nothing about his eyes changes.

One subject, three thresholds

There are as many just noticeable differences as there are levels of certainty you might demand. So the threshold is never a fact about a subject alone; it is a fact about a subject plus an unstated convention about how sure he must be before speaking. Fechner's procedure leaves that convention out of the report, which is exactly why the number looks like it belongs to the subject.

STAGE 01

The question, and the apparatus

Peirce and Jastrow saw that the two methods make incompatible predictions below the threshold. If a least perceptible difference exists, sub-threshold pairs must be judged wrongly as often as rightly. If the theory of errors is right, they must be judged rightly more often — and in a ratio calculable in advance.

The apparatus, December 1883 to April 1884. A Fairbanks post-office scale with a half-inch square plate fixed to the beam, capped in India rubber so the finger felt no cold metal. A kilogram in the pan delivered a quarter of its weight to the fingertip. The differential weight sat in a small cork-bottomed pan lowered by a stretched rubber thread, so that nothing jarred. A screen hid the operator. Peirce used his left forefinger, Jastrow his left middle finger. Fifty trials to a set, one hundred and fifty to a two-hour sitting.

And a detail that matters more than it looks: the order of each trial was set by a shuffled pack of twenty-five cards, twelve red and thirteen black, their corners punched to record the confidence mark. This is the first deliberately randomised experiment in the human sciences.

What randomisation buys you

The simulated subject is identical in both conditions. The only difference is that in the second an operator with unconscious habits — a slight tendency to alternate — hands the subject a pattern to lock onto. The estimate inflates, and no amount of care in the measurement itself would reveal it. The deck is not a nicety. It is what makes the observed proportion an estimate of discrimination rather than of pattern-learning.

It fits — down to a pressure ratio of 1.005, a difference of half a per cent. No corner appears anywhere, no floor at fifty per cent, and the fitted probable error keeps shrinking as the observers practise rather than settling at some fixed sensory limit. This is what they meant by destroying the presumption in favour of a threshold: not a refutation from principle but a curve that keeps on behaving.

STAGE 03

The innovation: recording confidence

On every trial the observer also marked how sure he was, on a four-point scale. Zero did not mean a weak impression. It meant no preference at all — that answering seemed pointless.

Jastrow's trials, split by confidence mark

Read along the confidence-zero row. At a pressure ratio of 1.005 — half a per cent — Jastrow was right on 294 of 497 trials he described as pure guesswork: 59.2 per cent. Peirce, across his own 706 zero-confidence trials, was right 61.8 per cent of the time; Jastrow across 1,123 such trials, 69.7 per cent. The judgements were affected by differences the observer had no awareness of noticing, which is why they concluded that the confidence itself has no threshold and fades only as the difference it refers to fades.

STAGE 04

Confidence as a measured regularity

They then proposed that the confidence mark varies as the logarithm of the odds of being right, with a constant they called the index of confidence. Fit it against their published means.

Mean confidence against the log-odds of a correct answer

A wrinkle worth knowing before you cite this. The two formulas in the paper use different logarithms. The error rule of footnote 2 needs natural logs to reproduce the published error tables; the confidence relation needs common logs to reproduce the published confidence columns — with natural logs it overshoots by a factor of 2.3 throughout. Neither formula says which is meant. On common logs the relation recovers ten of their twelve calculated values to within 0.05, and the two misses are cells where their own observed and calculated columns already disagree.
This is a different kind of commitment from Fechner's. The relation is offered as a regularity open to disconfirmation by the very data that support it, and they say plainly that the marks were rough and might be improved in later work. Compare the posit of a continuously varying sensation, which is required in advance so that two methods can be counted as measuring one thing, and which no operation in either method could test. Peirce theorises past his data here too — but the debt is one his experiment could call in.

CLOSING

What the confidence mark separated

Tab two measured a boundary: the point at which a difference became apparent. Tab one measured a capacity: how often the right answer comes up. Fechner treated these as two routes to a single quantity, and supplied the quantity — a sensation varying continuously beneath both — because without it the two methods have no common object.

Adding one column to the record was enough to pull them apart. The zero-confidence trials are exactly the ones the method of limits scores as nothing, and they are right well above chance. Discrimination and awareness came apart in the data, which is why Peirce concluded that we draw on impressions too faint for us to be aware of having, and left the underlying quantity alone.

THE WHOLE CHAIN

From a judgement to a magnitude of sensation

Both columns start with a person responding to a stimulus and end with a number said to measure his sensation. They pass through the same four way-stations and arrive at different values. What separates them is not accuracy but what each had to be given along the way.

THE BILL

What each method had to be given

The two columns divide unevenly. The distributional debt falls entirely on the method of right and wrong cases: it cannot convert a proportion into a magnitude without assuming a curve, and it cannot mark any point on that curve as a threshold without a criterion imported from outside. The method of limits owes neither — it reaches a full logarithmic scale of sensation with no error law anywhere in it. But the identity debt falls on both. Neither method contains any operation that could test whether a just noticeable difference at 20 grams is the same amount of sensation as one at 200, and without that, counting steps means nothing. That assumption is what makes the two methods appear to have a single object, and it is supplied rather than found in each of them.

WHERE IT BREAKS

The join that Peirce and Jastrow tested

Read across the first row of the pipeline. One method's raw datum is a boundary report — the point at which a difference becomes apparent. The other's is a forced choice — which of two is greater, apparent or not. Fechner's system requires these to be two windows onto one quantity, and supplies a continuously varying sensation to make them so.

The third tab is what happens when you record, on every trial, whether anything was apparent. The trials marked zero are precisely the ones the boundary method scores as nothing, and they are right well above chance. At that point the two columns below are no longer two routes to one number; they are measurements of two different things that a posit had been holding together.

SUMMER 1872 · 11,803 RESPONSES

The experiment that was meant to settle it

A young man of about eighteen, with no experience of scientific observation, sat for twenty-four days between the first of July and the third of August and responded to a sharp rap by depressing a telegraph key. A Hipp chronoscope recorded each response to the millisecond. Roughly five hundred responses a day. Peirce's question was whether errors of observation follow the Gaussian law — and therefore whether least squares is the right way to treat astronomical data.

The raw frequencies

What Peirce actually did, in his own description. The plotted curve is not drawn from the observations directly. The figures were smoothed by adding adjacent numbers in the table eight times over, to reduce the irregularities. Adding adjacent entries repeatedly is convolution with a binomial kernel: eight passes gives the eighth row of Pascal's triangle, a near-Gaussian weighting about 1.4 rows wide. The rows of his table are single milliseconds, which is the resolution used here — at roughly 1.5 responses per millisecond, eight passes leave about a third of the sampling noise standing, which is why his plotted curves oscillate as they do. Then — and this is the step that matters most — the smoother curve on each figure is a mean curve drawn by eye, to eliminate the irregularities entirely. No fitting, no least squares, no estimated parameters stand between the observations and the shape being judged. A hand does.

WILSON AND HILFERTY, PNAS, 1929

What the moments say

Fifty-seven years later, while preparing Peirce's official biography for the Academy, Edwin Wilson came across the experiment and looked again. He and Margaret Hilferty computed twenty-three statistics for every one of the twenty-four days, by hand. Their conclusion was the opposite of Peirce's.

As Peirce plotted it

Sorted observations against theoretical quantiles

A straight line means the law fits. Switch the reference between the two candidates: Laplace's second law, the Gaussian, which Peirce set out to confirm, and Laplace's first law, the double exponential, which Fréchet and then Wilson and Hilferty preferred. The Gaussian plot bends away at both ends on almost every day. The Laplace plot is straighter through much more of its range — not perfect, but the difference is not subtle.

Too peaked at the centre and too heavy in the tails, at once

The family being slid through is exp(\u2212|x/\u03b1|\u03b2). At \u03b2 = 2 it is the Gaussian, at \u03b2 = 1 Laplace's first law; below 1 it is heavier-tailed than either. Fit it to each day and the answer never once comes back near 2. The mean of the twenty-four maximum-likelihood estimates is 1.10, the range runs from 0.73 to 1.48, and Laplace outscores the Gaussian on likelihood on 22 of the 24 days. This is the modern restatement of what Wilson and Hilferty saw in their columns: the ratio of half the interquartile range to 0.6745\u03c3 would be 1 for a Gaussian and is below 1 every day, so the middle is tighter than the spread implies, while the count beyond 3.1\u03c3 is far above what a Gaussian permits. More concentrated at the centre and more extended at the extremes is the signature of a smaller \u03b2.

Across the twenty-four days

Gaussian kurtosis is 3 and Laplace is 6; the mean across Peirce's days is 27.5. Using the tests Koenker applies, Gaussian skewness is rejected on 19 of the 24 days and Gaussian kurtosis on all 24. Watch the second half of the series in particular: as the observer settles down and his spread narrows, the excursions do not go away, so the kurtosis climbs rather than falls.

KOENKER, THE AMERICAN STATISTICIAN, 2009

The median is the message

Koenker set out to evaluate Wilson and Hilferty's methods against later statistical developments, and to see how much of a hand computation from 1929 could be reproduced. His verdict on their central claim is that it is entirely vindicated: for Peirce's data the median is a competitive estimator, and normality is implausible.

Daily means and medians, with pointwise error bands

The day-to-day movement swamps everything inside a day. The first week's fall is learning \u2014 the young man gets faster with practice. The slow climb afterwards is harder to account for; Koenker wonders about ennui. Either way the error bands for the mean and the median are indistinguishable at this scale, which is his point: the median costs you nothing here. Switch to the quantile view and the asymmetry becomes visible directly \u2014 the upper quantiles wander far more than the lower ones, because the long right tail is where the instability lives.

Which estimator would you actually want?

Koenker frames it as mean against median. There is a whole road between them: trim a fraction from each end before averaging, and you get the mean at 0 and the median at one half. Four hundred bootstrap replications per day, per setting.

Bootstrap standard error, by day

The untrimmed mean is not the best estimator on a single one of the twenty-four days. Trimming a tenth from each end wins outright on ten of them, a twentieth on five, a quarter on six, and the median on three. So the honest summary is slightly stronger than the one Koenker states: it is not merely that the median is competitive with the mean, but that almost any modest trimming beats the mean everywhere, which is what Stigler's 1977 survey of robust estimators on real data would lead you to expect. The mean is the one choice the data never support.

The column nobody can reconstruct

Wilson and Hilferty's argument turns on comparing the precision of the median against the mean. But the phrase "standard deviation of the median" had no settled meaning in 1929, and Koenker tried seven reconstructions. Toggle them against the values they published.

Standard deviation of the median: theirs, and seven attempts at it

How close each reconstruction comes

All of them support their conclusion. None of them reproduces their numbers. The Laplace route \u2014 taking their faith in the first law wholeheartedly and using the mean absolute deviation \u2014 is much too small, averaging 1.16 against their 1.54. Yule's textbook density estimate and Siddiqui's sparsity function both come close but need bandwidths far smaller than anything a 1929 reader would have chosen. The bootstrap, which they could not possibly have used, fits best of all. The question stands open.
Why this matters beyond the arithmetic. Peirce ran the experiment to license least squares, and least squares is optimal under the Gaussian law. Laplace's first law licenses the median instead. So the question of which law fits is not idle curiosity about a shape: it decides which estimator the whole apparatus of nineteenth-century observational astronomy should have been built on. Peirce concluded the Gaussian was verified. Fr\u00e9chet, Wilson and Hilferty, and Koenker all read the same numbers the other way.
