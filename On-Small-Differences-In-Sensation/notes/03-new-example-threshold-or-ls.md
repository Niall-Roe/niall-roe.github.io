### Text

"Accordingly we find that the frequencies of errors of different magnitudes follow the probability curve, which is the law of an effect brought about by the sum of an infinite number of infinitesimal causes. This theory, however, does not admit of an Unterschiedsschwelle. On the contrary, it leads to the method of least squares, according to which the multiplication of observations will indefinitely reduce the error of their mean, so that if of two excitations one were ever so little the more intense, in the long run it would be judged to be the more intense the majority of times."

#### the two predictions, and what it costs to tell them apart

Built to both halves of your note.

**The curve.** Proportion judged right against the difference in weight, in
parts per thousand — the units Jastrow's differences are quoted in later (60, 30
and 15 on the thousand). Least squares is the ogive: an even chance at no
difference, rising steadily. The threshold is drawn over it, flat at chance up
to the Schwelle and identical to least squares above it. Three sliders: the
difference, the threshold claimed, and the observer's error.

The point falls out of the drawing. Above the threshold the two curves lie on
top of one another and nothing can tell them apart. Below it they separate, and
the readout gives both numbers.

**The distribution**, underneath, is the same disagreement the other way round:
the observer's error laid over the true difference, with the part falling the
right side of nothing shaded — that shaded area is the proportion above — and
the threshold's band drawn round nothing as the region in which it says no
judgment is possible.

**What it costs.** The trials figure you asked for: how many are needed to show
the observer beats chance nineteen times in twenty. At 8 in 1000 it is 61
trials; at 2 in 1000 it is 945. Against the threshold's prediction no number of
trials will do, because its prediction is chance. That is the argument for why
the memoir counts in the thousands, and it is now on the page rather than
implied.

One thing worth flagging: at a difference of exactly nothing the first version
said the two theories "part company" and that least squares predicted "a
majority, however slight". Both false — at no difference both say an even
chance, and nothing is not a difference. Since zero is the first thing a reader
will slide to, that is now its own case and says so.
