The method of average error

The subject moves the weight himself, and stops when it seems right.

In the first two methods the experimenter chose the weights. Here the subject holds the dial. He moves the comparison until it seems right to him, and where he stops is written down. Then it is set to a new starting point and he does it again. What is recorded is not an answer to a question but a setting he produced.

Part one · the absolute threshold

Step 1 · one setting

Watch him settle

He is asked to make the weight on his palm as light as he can while still feeling it. The trace shows the dial as he works: he overshoots, comes back, and settles.

The dial during one setting. Only the resting value at the end is recorded.

Step 2 · the record

A column of settings

Every setting so far. They scatter; none is the threshold on its own.

Step 3 · the threshold

Take the mean

A note on this task This method is often said to give no absolute threshold, because “adjust until it matches” needs something to match. That is true of the matching task in part two. Asked instead to find the faintest weight he can still feel, he can do it, and the mean of his settings is the threshold directly, with no curve and no chosen proportion.

Part two · the difference threshold

Step 1 · one setting

Match the standard

Now he adjusts the comparison until it feels equal to the 100 g standard. Each setting begins from a different starting point, above or below, so he cannot simply repeat himself.

The dial during one matching setting. The dashed line is the true 100 g.

Step 2 · the record

Settings and their errors

Each setting is written down with how far it fell from the standard. The second column is the one Fechner's name for the method refers to.

The settings, their mean, and the band holding the middle half of them.

Step 3 · two numbers from the scatter

Where the middle is, and how wide it is

The middle of the settings and the width of the settings answer different questions, and both are wanted.

What rides along A setting is a movement of the hand as well as a judgement of the senses, so unsteadiness adds to the scatter and is counted as dullness. That is why this method tends to give a slightly larger difference threshold than the first one. With only forty settings the difference is smaller than the uncertainty of the estimate itself; take two hundred to see it separate.
