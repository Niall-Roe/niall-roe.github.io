Fechner's three methods

A subject sits at a bench with brass weights. Five pages working through how what he says becomes a number.

Gustav Fechner set out three procedures for measuring a sense in Elemente der Psychophysik (1860). Each page here takes one of them: what the subject is asked to do, the record that produces, and the arithmetic that turns that record into a threshold. The last two pages put the results side by side and let you run the methods on yourself.

Two thresholds

Every method below is used to find two different quantities, and it is worth keeping them apart from the start.

The absolute threshold

Weights are laid on the subject's palm, one at a time. Very light ones he does not feel at all. The absolute threshold, Fechner's Reizlimen or RL, is the weight at which sensation begins. Because his answer at any one weight varies from trial to trial, it is taken as the weight he feels half the time.

The difference threshold

A 100 g standard sits in one hand and a comparison weight in the other. Small differences he cannot tell apart. The difference threshold, the Unterschiedsschwelle or DL, is the smallest change he can notice. It is the same idea as the just noticeable difference.

Two further quantities come out of the comparison task and are worth naming now, because two of the methods report them:

  • The point of subjective equality is the comparison weight that feels equal to the standard. There is no guarantee it is 100 g.
  • The constant error is how far that point sits from the true 100 g. It is steady across sessions and depends on things like which hand holds the standard.

The subject

The subject on these pages is simulated, so that the answer is known while you work. Five numbers describe him. The first two are his senses. The last two are not, and that is what makes the three methods disagree later.

Nothing in these figures has been chosen to make the methods agree. Where they agree it is because they are recovering the same quantity; where they differ, the page says which part of the procedure the difference comes from.

How he answers

Set a weight on his palm and see how often he says he feels it. The curve is his detection probability; the bars are what actually happened on the trials you ran.

His chance of feeling a weight on the palm, against the weight. The absolute threshold is where the curve passes one half.

What is on the other pages
