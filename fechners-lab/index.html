    Fechner's laboratory

    A subject sits at a bench lifting brass weights. Fechner wanted a number for the smallest difference that subject can feel — and devised three procedures to get one. Each tab below walks one method through: what it asks the subject to do, what it records, how a threshold is squeezed out of that, and then the same method run on you.

    BEFORE YOU START

    One quantity, three procedures

    The target is the difference threshold: how much heavier a comparison weight must be before the subject can tell it apart from a 100 g standard. It sounds like a single fact about him. Fechner's three methods each go after it differently, and none of them observes it directly — every one produces some other raw quantity (a proportion, a crossing point, a spread of settings) and then applies a rule to turn that into a threshold.

    Work through the tabs in any order. Each ends with you as the subject, so by the closing section you will have three numbers of your own to compare.

    METHOD ONE

    Right and wrong cases

    Fix a small set of differences in advance. Present them over and over in random order. Score every response simply right or wrong, and count. The subject is never asked how much heavier, never asked to adjust anything — he classifies, and you tally.

    Notice what a single trial contained. Not a magnitude — just a direction. Yet after a few hundred trials the proportion has settled to a stable number that no individual judgement carried. Fechner saw this clearly: in this method "each individual observation has next to no meaning," and a handful of trials, however carefully made, give nothing at all. Compare an astronomer's transit timing, where every observation is already a measurement and averaging merely sharpens it.

    Step two — vary the difference

    Repeat that procedure at several comparison weights. Each button runs 120 trials at one difference and plots the result.

    Proportion correct against difference

    Before reading on — if the subject had a genuine threshold, a difference below which he truly cannot tell, what shape should these points make?

    A true threshold predicts a corner. Below it, chance performance — a flat line at 0.5. Above it, rising. The data have no corner: performance climbs smoothly out of chance from the very smallest difference. A hard cutoff cannot produce that shape, which is why the next step reaches for a probability curve instead of a step — and why this method will need a stipulated percentage to point at and call "the threshold."

    Step three — what would produce that curve?

    Suppose each lift registers internally as the true weight plus a random error. Then two lifts give two noisy values, and the subject simply reports whichever felt larger. He is right whenever the noise doesn't overturn the true ordering. Tune the noise until the prediction matches what you collected.

    METHOD TWO

    Limits

    Now the experimenter holds the controls. Instead of scattering fixed differences at random, he moves the comparison in ordered steps and asks after each one: different, or the same? The subject never sees a random jump — he feels a ladder being climbed or descended beneath him. That single change to the procedure changes what the raw data are, what can go wrong, and what rule is needed to reach a threshold.

    Step one — walk down a single series

    Start the comparison well above the standard, where the difference is obvious, and step it down half a gram at a time. After each step the subject answers. Present the steps one at a time and watch for the moment his answer flips.

    One series yields exactly one number. Not a proportion — a transition point, and by convention it is the midpoint between the last "different" and the first "same", because the truth lies somewhere in the half-gram you stepped over. Note how little you had to do: a handful of trials, and a threshold-shaped number falls out immediately. That economy is the method's great attraction, and its trap. You have one observation of one crossing on one occasion, and nothing yet tells you how much it would move if you did it again.

    Step two — the direction you came from matters

    Run the mirror image: start below anything detectable and step up until he first says "different." If the crossing were a fixed property of the subject, the two directions would land on the same number.

    Crossings so far, by direction

    They disagree, and not randomly. Descending series cross low; ascending series cross high. The subject carries momentum: having said "different" four times he is disposed to say it once more, and having said "same" he keeps saying it. That is the habituation error. Its opposite, anticipation, appears when he knows what is coming and jumps early. Both are artifacts of presenting stimuli in a known order — the price of the method's economy.

    Step three — build up a set

    One series of each is not evidence. Run fourteen more, alternating direction, and watch two distinct clouds form. The slider lets you dial the subject's habituation up and down; re-run and see what moves.

    What was assumed. This method never computes a proportion, so it never needs a stipulated percentage the way method one did. In its place it assumes the two directions err symmetrically — that habituation pushes ascending up exactly as far as it pushes descending down. Nothing in the data establishes that. Method one smuggled in a curve and a criterion; limits smuggles in a symmetry. Both are the experimenter's contribution, not the subject's.

    Now allow a third answer. Each sweep now turns over twice, and those two transitions bracket something new.

    One procedure, three quantities — and they are not the same quantity in different clothes. The band where he answers "equal" is the interval of uncertainty: the whole zone in which the comparison is indistinguishable from the standard. Its midpoint is the point of subjective equality — where "equal" sits for him, which need not be where physics puts it. Half its width is the JND. So the same twenty minutes at the bench yields a location (PSE), a width (IU), and a sensitivity (JND), each read off the same two transition points by a different arithmetic rule. Change the rule and you have changed which threshold you measured.

    METHOD THREE

    Adjustment

    Hand the controls to the subject. No fixed levels, no ladder, no yes-or-no: he turns a screw that moves the comparison continuously, and stops when it seems equal to the standard. You write down where he stopped. The raw datum is a setting — not a judgement about a stimulus you chose, but an action he produced.

    Step one — take a single setting

    He adjusts until the two feel the same, and stops. One number. Take it.

    Error—
    He did not stop at 100. He stopped near it, and he was certain it looked right. On its own that number tells you almost nothing: you cannot say whether the miss is a quirk of this one attempt or something systematic about him. Unlike method one, where a single trial was obviously worthless, here a single observation looks like a measurement — which makes it more misleading, not less.
    Now the shape of the data appears. Every setting misses, and they miss by different amounts in different directions. This is the astronomer's situation exactly: repeated observations of one quantity, scattering. Fechner's move is to treat the subject as an instrument and read the scatter the way an astronomer reads the scatter of transit timings — which is why this method's older name is the method of average error.

    Step two — two numbers from one scatter

    With forty settings there is a distribution to describe, and it takes two numbers to describe it. They answer different questions and neither substitutes for the other.

    The centre is a location; the width is a sensitivity. The mean is the point of subjective equality — where "equal" sits for him. Its gap from the true 100 g is a constant error, and it is stubbornly reproducible: which hand held the standard, which weight he lifted first. The spread is the sensitivity measure, and the threshold is taken as a fixed multiple of it. The two are independent. A subject can be beautifully precise and reliably wrong about where equality lies, and this method is the one that shows you both at once.
    What was assumed. The same move as method one wearing different clothes: a dispersion becomes a threshold only by multiplying it by 0.6745 — the probable error, the distance containing the middle half of the settings. Method one needed a percentage on a curve; this needs a multiple of a spread. Note also what this method cannot do at all: "adjust until it matches" presupposes something to match, so there is no way here to find the faintest detectable weight. It gives a difference threshold and a PSE, and no absolute threshold whatever.
    The contamination it invites. A setting is a produced movement, so motor error rides into the number alongside sensory error, and nothing in the data separates them. A subject with a heavy hand on the screw looks less sensitive than he is.

    The simulated subject (grams)

    You (grey levels)

    Why they are close, and why that is not proof of anything. The simulated subject converges because he was built with one criterion written into him — a single σ that all three procedures interrogate. A real subject offers no such guarantee, and your own three numbers may well disagree more. What separates the methods is what each asks the subject to do (classify, report a crossing, produce a setting) and what each then needs before its raw data will yield a threshold at all: a stipulated percentage, an assumption of symmetric error, or a multiple of a dispersion. Those rules were chosen by the experimenter, not discovered in the subject.

    WHAT A THRESHOLD BUYS YOU

    Does the noise stay put?

    Nothing so far says the subject behaves the same at other weights. Run the whole procedure again at each standard and see.

    Threshold, and threshold as a fraction of the standard

    The absolute figures scatter across an order of magnitude; the ratio does not move. That constancy is Weber's law, and it is an empirical finding, not a definition — this subject could have come out otherwise. It is also what makes a general scale possible: if the threshold were an arbitrary function of the standard there would be nothing to count. Fechner named the law after Weber and then built everything on top of it.

    WHAT A THRESHOLD BUYS YOU

    Counting your way to a scale

    Here is Fechner's move. Declare one threshold to be one unit of sensation — the same size wherever it occurs, because a just noticeable difference is a just noticeable difference. Now start at the faintest detectable weight and climb, one unit at a time.

    Each unit is a fixed ratio, so the steps grow

    You did not assume a logarithm; you counted, and a logarithm appeared. Equal sensation steps are equal ratios of stimulus, so the physical steps stretch as you climb, and the count against the weight traces a log curve. That is Fechner's law. Notice also what fixed the bottom of the scale: the absolute threshold, the faintest detectable weight. Move that starting point and every sensation magnitude in the system moves with it.

    CLOSING

    What you built, and what you assumed

    Reading down the notebook: judgements gave proportions; proportions plus an assumed error curve gave a dispersion; a dispersion plus a chosen criterion gave a threshold; a threshold plus Weber's law gave a unit; a unit plus a starting point gave a scale of sensation running to about a hundred units at 100 g.

    Two of those links are measurements and three are decisions. The subject supplied the proportions, the constancy of the ratio, and the transition points. The experimenter supplied the shape of the error curve, the criterion that turns a smooth function into a threshold, and the stipulation that a just noticeable difference is everywhere the same size. All five are needed to reach a number, and the number does not announce which of its parents was which.
