Fechner's three methods

A subject lifts brass weights. Three ways of turning what he says into a number.

Elemente der Psychophysik, 1860

Fechner wanted to measure the smallest difference a subject can feel. A 100 g weight sits in one hand and a slightly heavier one in the other. How much heavier does the second have to be before he notices?

He described three ways of finding out. Each asks the subject to do something different, and each records something different. None of them observes a threshold. Each produces some other quantity and then applies a rule to turn it into one.

The method of just noticeable differences

Here the experimenter moves the comparison in small steps and asks after each one: different, or the same? The subject feels a ladder being climbed or descended. Later writers call this the method of limits.

One series gives one number. It cost a handful of trials, which is why the method was popular. But you have one crossing on one occasion, and nothing yet says how much it would move if you ran it again.
Run another and the direction reverses. Descending series cross low, ascending series cross high. He carries his last answer forward a step or two before switching. Fechner called this habituation.

Raise his habituation and the two rows separate. The average of the two barely moves. Neither direction on its own estimates anything you want; each is the threshold plus a bias, with the sign reversed between them.
The rule. Take the mean of all crossings, both directions together. This method needs no chosen percentage. It assumes instead that the two directions err by the same amount in opposite directions. Nothing in the data shows that.

The method of average error

Now the subject holds the controls. He moves the comparison until it matches the standard and you write down where he stopped. What you record is a setting, not an answer to a question.

He did not stop at 100, and he was sure it looked right. One setting looks like a measurement, which makes it more misleading than a single right-or-wrong trial, not less. You cannot yet tell whether the miss is a quirk or a habit.
Take more and the shape appears. Every setting misses, by different amounts in both directions. This is the astronomer's situation: repeated observations of one quantity, scattering around it.

The middle is a location. The width is a sensitivity. The mean is where "equal" sits for him, and its distance from 100 g is a constant error, which stays put across sessions: which hand held the standard, which weight he lifted first. The threshold comes from the width alone. A subject can be precise and reliably wrong at the same time.
The rule. The same move as the first method in different dress: multiply the spread by 0.6745 and call the result a threshold. Note also what this method cannot do. "Adjust until it matches" needs something to match, so there is no way here to find the faintest weight he can feel at all.
What rides along. A setting is a movement, so unsteady hands add to the spread and count as dullness.

What a threshold is for

A threshold on its own is a small fact about one weight. Fechner wanted a scale of sensation, and two further steps got him there.

The same subject at other weights

Nothing so far says he behaves the same way at 25 g or 400 g. Run the whole procedure again at each standard.

The thresholds in grams range over an order of magnitude. As a fraction of the standard they hardly move. That is Weber's law. It is a finding, not a definition: he could have come out otherwise.
Counting

Now Fechner's step. Call one threshold one unit of sensation, the same size wherever it falls. Start at the faintest weight he can feel and climb, one unit at a time.

You counted, and a logarithm appeared. Equal steps of sensation are equal ratios of weight, so the physical steps grow as you climb. That is Fechner's law. Note what set the bottom of the scale: the faintest weight he can feel. Move that and every number on the scale moves.

What was measured, and what was decided

Reading back: answers gave proportions; proportions and an assumed curve gave a spread; a spread and a chosen fraction gave a threshold; a threshold and Weber's law gave a unit; a unit and a starting point gave a scale.

Two of those steps are measurements. Three are decisions. The subject supplied the proportions, the constancy of the ratio, and the crossings. The experimenter supplied the shape of the error curve, the fraction that turns a curve into a threshold, and the rule that a just noticeable difference is the same size wherever it occurs. All five are needed to reach a number, and the number does not say which of them it came from.
