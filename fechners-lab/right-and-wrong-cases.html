The method of right and wrong cases

Fixed weights, presented over and over in random order. Count the answers.

Choose a few weights in advance and keep them fixed. Present them many times, in random order so the subject cannot anticipate. Record his answer each time. Nothing is adjusted during the experiment and the subject is never asked how much — only which, or whether.

Step 4 · the threshold

Fit a curve and read it off

There is no weight in the table at which he changes from never to always, so no row is the threshold. Instead a normal ogive is fitted to the points, and the threshold is taken as the weight it crosses at one half.

The fitted curve, with the half-way crossing dropped to the axis.

What was assumed The shape of the curve, and that one half is the right place to read. Neither comes from the subject. Fit a different curve, or read at a different proportion, and the threshold moves.

Step 4 · the thresholds

Three readings off one curve

Fit the ogive again. This time three quantities are read from it, at different heights.

The half-way crossing gives the point of subjective equality; the three-quarter crossing is one difference threshold above it.

Why three quarters The distance from the half-way point to the three-quarter point is the probable error of the fitted curve: the amount either side of the middle that holds half of his judgements. It is a convention for summarising the spread, adopted from astronomy. It is not something the subject did.
