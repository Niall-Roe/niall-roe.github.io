Regression to the Mean

Galton had a quincunx built in 1873: a funnel, rows of offset pins, and a row of compartments at the bottom, so that shot poured in at the top settled into a normal curve. It showed how a heap of small accidental deflections makes the shape Quetelet found everywhere. What it did not show was how that shape survives heredity, where the causes are neither small nor accidental. The machine below is the answer Galton worked out on the quincunx, and it is interactive because he seems never to have built it.

Cut the board in two part-way down and put a second row of compartments at the cut, the level marked A–A. Barriers run from there down into the lower half so that nothing strays sideways while it crosses. Now the shot stop half way. Open one compartment at A–A and its contents fall through the remaining pins into a small normal heap of their own, directly below where they started. Open the compartments one at a time and each makes its own little heap, the central ones larger because more shot stopped there. When every compartment has been opened, the heaps have added up to exactly the curve the uninterrupted machine would have made.

That is the first thing to try on the board below. Push r to 1, so that the barriers hang straight as he drew them — the board opens with them slanted, which is the later part of the story — then click a single compartment in the upper row and watch its heap. Let the rest follow, and watch the sum come out the shape it would have been anyway. A normal population is a mixture of normal families, and the mixture gives nothing away about the parts.

What the barriers cannot do

Read the machine as heredity and something is wrong with it. Each generation is a whole population, spread σ; each family scatters about its parent by the lower bank's own spread σf; so the children come out at √(σ² + σf²), wider than their parents, and wider again the generation after, without limit. Set r to 1 so the barriers hang straight down, and press Drop a generation a dozen times: the population spreads until the shot are piling against the walls of the board. Stature does not do that.

Galton's answer was to set the barriers on a slant. Each carries its compartment's contents a fraction 1 − r of the way back toward the mean before releasing it, so a family whose parent stands d from the mean is centred not on d but on r d, and the population comes out at √(r²σ² + σf²). Set the lower bank to σf = σ√(1 − r²) and the two terms cancel exactly: the children are spread precisely as far as their parents were, and the machine can be run on its own output for ever — ticking Hold the population still keeps the lower bank at that setting while r moves. Galton called r the coefficient of reversion in 1877 and renamed it regression in 1885, putting it near 0.67 for the stature of children on their mid-parents. The first slider is that coefficient. At its present setting, 0.67, a compartment +2.00 σ from the mean sends its children to +1.34 σ, and the population on the board stands at 1.00 σ. With the hold ticked, the scatter within families refills the tails exactly as fast as the chutes drain them, and that last number stops moving from one generation to the next.

Which way you read it

It is tempting to read the fan of chutes as a force: something in the apparatus dragging each generation toward the middle. The board refuses the reading, and it refuses it with the barriers hanging straight. Set r to 1, drop a generation, and click a compartment in the lower row — the following switches to reading backwards on its own. Its shot fell straight down, so nothing regressed on the way; yet the parents they came from lie toward the centre of the board, because there were always more shot near the centre to have come from. Stigler puts the two questions side by side: where a shot released from A–A is expected to land, and where a shot lying at the bottom is expected to have come from. The first answer is “directly below”. The second is not “directly above”.

Nothing pulls the shot toward the middle, then: the backward answer lies where it does because of where the shot stood before they fell. In the straight machine that is the whole of the regression, and it runs backwards only — forwards, a compartment's children land under it. Slant the chutes and it runs both ways at once, and the two readings agree exactly while the spread holds still from one generation to the next: children +2.00 σ from their generation’s mean come from parents averaging +1.34 σ from theirs, the same fraction the chutes carry every family back.

What it says about the mean: nothing

The chutes are set on the mean of whatever is in the compartments, not on any fixed mark: what a family reverts toward is its own population, wherever that population has got to. So the apparatus governs the shape of the distribution about the mean and has no purchase on the mean itself. Move the environment slider and every child is displaced by the same amount before it falls — better feeding, in Galton's terms, applied to all alike. Run twenty generations and the mean walks steadily off across the board while the spread does not move at all: the two lines in the lower figure separate completely. A trait can be strongly heritable, in exactly the sense the chutes make precise, and still have a mean that goes wherever circumstances take it.

Reading the apparatus. The upper bank fills the compartments at A–A only when you ask for a new population; after that each generation is the one before it, lifted from the lower row into the upper. A compartment that has been opened is left as a pale ghost of what it held, so it is always visible which are still to go. The outline held over the children is their parents’, so the two heaps can be compared where they stand.

The board has walls. Shot that would land beyond the end compartments pile up in them instead, which is why the histogram grows shoulders once the population outruns the board; the spread and mean reported underneath, and plotted by generation below that, are computed from where the shot would have landed and not from where the walls put them.

The apparatus and the argument are Galton’s. The two-stage quincunx is described in correspondence from 1877 and figured in Natural Inheritance (1889), p. 63; Stigler notes that there is no sign Galton ever built it, and reports that building one that works is very hard indeed. The release of the compartments one at a time is Galton’s thought experiment as Stigler reconstructs it; the question asked in both directions is Stigler’s way of showing what it proves; any errors in the implementation are this page’s own.
