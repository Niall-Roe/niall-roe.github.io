How to write it

~ & | -> <-> for not, and, or, if-then, if-and-only-if. The glyphs ¬ ∧ ∨ ⊃ ≡ work too.

Ax and Ex for ∀x and ∃x; (x) and (Ex) also work.

Predicates take a capital: Fx, Axy, loves(x,y). Identity is x=y.

Peirce allows no graph with an unoccupied hook, so a free variable cannot be scribed at all (Roberts p. 49). One is closed existentially for you, with a note saying so.

Linear notation writes down the graph itself, mark by mark, instead of a formula it stands for.

( ) is a cut. Whatever you put inside it is denied, so ( P ) is not P.

Whatever is written side by side is asserted together, so P Q is P and Q, and ( P ( Q ) ) is if P then Q.

A capital letter or a word is a spot: P, Man.

Lines of identity get small letters. *x puts a point of the line x where you write it, and F[x] is a spot with its hook on x, so *x F[x] is something is F. A spot with more hooks lists them in order: L[x,y].

A line named inside a cut passes through the cut, and where you first name it decides how it reads: ( *x ( F[x] ) ) is everything is F.

x~y joins two lines into one, and { P | Q } is short for ( P ( Q ) ).

The conventions

… this act, called scribing a graph on the sheet of assertion, shall be understood to constitute the assertion of the truth of the graph scribed.Peirce, CP 4.397

Writing something on the sheet asserts it. The blank sheet asserts nothing, but it is still a graph.

Alpha and Beta

Alpha uses two marks. Write two graphs side by side and you have asserted both: Two graphs scribed on the sheet are, both of them, asserted (CP 4.398). Draw a cut round a graph and you have denied it; the cut severs all that it encloses from the sheet it stands on (CP 4.399). Those two marks handle every truth function. Whether an Alpha inference holds can always be settled by checking every assignment, so this page never has to guess.

Beta adds one more mark, the heavy line. Every heavily marked point shall denote a single individual, without in itself indicating what individual it is (CP 4.405), and a line joining two such points asserts precisely the identity of the individuals denoted by its extremities (CP 4.406). That gives you relations and quantifiers. A line in an evenly enclosed place says some; the same line oddly enclosed says any. Where a line crosses a cut, the scope of the quantifier is drawn on the page instead of being fixed by an ordering convention. Beta is undecidable, so here a countermodel can show an inference fails, but a search that finds nothing proves nothing.

Peirce went further, to Gamma: modality, the broken cut, graphs about graphs. That part is not on this page.

The rules of manipulation

Let [the student] read these permissions and the commentary as he would listen to the rules of a new and intricate game, very closely attentive, but wide awake to the purpose of preparing for a lively and lightsome contest. Peirce, quoted in Roberts 1973, p. 14

You are permitted to write and erase certain aspects of the graphs. There are three rules for each, and they mirror one another. This is best understood with the interactive examples below, but here is an overview.

You are (R1) able to erase any graph that is on an even, or positive, surface. You are (R2) allowed to insert any graph you like into an odd, or negative, surface.

If you have a copy of a graph, you are (R3) allowed to write it again anywhere further in, in any area that its own area contains, however many cuts deep. You are (R4) allowed to erase any copy that could have been made that way.

You are (R5) allowed to draw two cuts, one just inside the other, around any graph, anywhere, or around nothing at all. You are (R6) allowed to take such a pair off again, as long as there is nothing between the two cuts.
