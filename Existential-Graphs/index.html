The Existential Graphs of Charles S. Peirce

A working instrument for Peirce's diagrammatic logic: translate ordinary notation into graphs, watch known proofs unfold, scribe graphs and read them back, and set the five rules of transformation to work on a problem of your own. Built on Don D. Roberts, The Existential Graphs of Charles S. Peirce (The Hague: Mouton, 1973), with Peirce's MS 514 as transcribed by John Sowa.

A proposition in ordinary notation

How to write it

~ & | -> <-> for not, and, or, if-then, if-and-only-if. The glyphs ¬ ∧ ∨ ⊃ ≡ work too.

Ax and Ex for ∀x and ∃x; (x) and (Ex) also work.

Predicates take a capital: Fx, Axy, loves(x,y). Identity is x=y.

Peirce allows no graph with an unoccupied hook, so a free variable cannot be scribed at all (Roberts p. 49). One is closed existentially for you, with a note saying so.

The permissions

Peirce's own name for the five rules, and his own advice on how to take them: Let [the student] read these permissions and the commentary as he would listen to the rules of a new and intricate game, very closely attentive, but wide awake to the purpose of preparing for a lively and lightsome contest. Peirce, quoted in Roberts 1973, p. 14

Each is illustrated below in the fewest marks that show it. Choose an illustration to set it up, then play it. Roberts calls them the rules of transformation (Appendix 3, p. 138); they are the same five.
